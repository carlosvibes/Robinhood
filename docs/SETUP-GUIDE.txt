PART 4 — RUNNING IT ON A SCHEDULE (WEEK 2+)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

READ THIS BEFORE AUTOMATING ANYTHING.

I originally wrote a cron job for this section and then caught the problem while
stress-testing. I'm leaving the explanation in because it's the kind of mistake
that looks fine until it costs you money.

THE TRAP: the obvious command is

    claude -p "run a trading session"

The -p flag is ONE-SHOT. Claude answers once and the process exits. It does not
sit there from 9:30 to 4:00 managing your positions. So cron fires at 8:30, the
AI thinks for a few minutes, possibly BUYS SOMETHING, and then quits — leaving a
live position with nobody watching it, all day, through the close. That is the
single worst outcome the whole system can produce, and it would happen silently
every morning.

WHAT TO DO INSTEAD: keep a real session alive.

  1. Turn off sleep during market hours.
       Mac:      System Settings > Lock Screen > turn display off never,
                 and run: caffeinate -d
       Windows:  Settings > System > Power > Screen and sleep > Never

  2. Open a terminal at 8:30am ET each market day:
       cd ~/trading
       claude

  3. Give it the session prompt:
       Load the robinhood-day-trader skill and run a full trading session for
       today. Phase 1 rules. Stay active until the flatten time.

  4. LEAVE THE WINDOW OPEN until 4:05pm ET. Check in on it periodically.

This is manual, and that's appropriate for now. An unattended process holding
positions it cannot manage is worse than no automation at all.

A SAFETY BACKSTOP worth adding either way — a one-shot job that only ever
closes things. This one IS reasonable as a -p command, because closing
positions is a single action that either completes or doesn't:

    Mac, in crontab -e (cron uses your machine's LOCAL time — set this to
    3:55pm in your own timezone, and re-check it after every daylight saving
    change, because cron will not adjust itself):

    55 15 * * 1-5 cd ~/trading && $(which claude) -p "Emergency flatten check. First check marketHours for today: if the market is closed or already closed more than 30 minutes ago, do nothing and exit. Otherwise query my Robinhood agentic account positions. If any position is open, cancel any resting stop orders first, verify the cancellation, then close the position with a marketable limit order. Verify by re-querying that position count is zero. Log the result to trade_ledger.md." >> ~/trading/flatten.log 2>&1

Check your path first with:  which claude

THREE THINGS TO KNOW ABOUT THIS BACKSTOP:

  - It runs 5 minutes AFTER the agent's own flatten, so it only acts if the
    main session already failed. That gap is deliberate.
  - The marketHours check at the front is what stops it from firing on
    holidays, or three hours after a 1:00pm half-day close.
  - It may hang waiting for a tool-permission prompt that nobody is there to
    answer. TEST IT on a day when you hold no positions and confirm it writes
    to flatten.log. An untested backstop is worse than none, because you'll
    believe it's protecting you.

  On a 1:00pm half-day the marketHours guard makes it no-op, so a half-day is
  the one session with no backstop. Half-days are published in advance — check
  the market calendar and either sit those out or watch the session yourself.

Do not add any of this until Step 10 has run cleanly for at least a week.

THE ANTI-HALLUCINATION CHECK guards the failure mode you can't see coming: an
AI can write a flawless thesis about a price it half-remembered, and the order
still goes through.

Before every order the agent must write its analysis price to the log FIRST,
then pull a live quote, confirm the ticker resolves, confirm the quote is less
than 30 seconds old, and confirm the live price is within 1% of both the logged
analysis price AND the setup's trigger level. Any failure means no trade.

The ordering is the whole trick. My first draft had it fetch the quote and then
record it as the "analysis price" — which compares a number to itself and passes
100% of the time. It would have looked like a working safety check while doing
absolutely nothing. Writing the number down first is what makes it real.

THE STOP-ORDER RULE is the other one worth knowing. No position may be opened
unless a resting stop order can actually be placed at the broker right after the
entry fills. If the stop is rejected, the position is closed immediately. A stop
that exists only in the AI's reasoning is not a stop — it depends on the session
staying alive, and sessions end.

The stop must be a STOP-MARKET order, not stop-limit. A stop-limit can go
unfilled exactly when you need it — a gap, a halt reopening, a sudden air
pocket. You accept a slightly worse fill in exchange for the fill actually
happening. This is the one place in the whole system where certainty is worth
more than price.

APPENDIX — WHAT THE STRESS TEST CHANGED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

I had a second AI attack the first draft of this system looking specifically for
ways it could lose money. It found real problems. Listing them because you asked
to learn the mechanics, and because these are the failure modes to watch for in
any AI trading setup, not just this one:

  1. FRACTIONAL SHARES CAN'T HOLD STOPS. Original sizing produced fractional
     positions, which typically can't carry a stop order. Fix: whole shares,
     $5-$10 price band, and a hard rule that no position opens without a
     resting broker-side stop.

  2. THE CRON JOB LEFT POSITIONS UNMANAGED. Covered in Part 4.

  3. THE ANTI-HALLUCINATION CHECK WAS CIRCULAR. Covered in Part 5.

  4. THE OPENING RANGE BREAKOUT COULD NEVER HIT 2:1. My original entry was on
     the breakout candle close. Run the geometry: with a stop at the range
     midpoint and a target one range-height above, you get exactly 2:1 only if
     you enter AT the range high. Every cent above it cuts reward and widens
     risk simultaneously. Entering on the breakout candle gave about 1.1:1 —
     meaning the AI would either take bad trades or fudge the numbers to pass
     its own rule. Fix: enter on the pullback retest, and compute the ratio
     explicitly before every order.

  5. TRADING HALTS WERE MISSING ENTIRELY. A halted stock can't be sold at any
     price, and stops don't execute through halts. This is the biggest tail
     risk in the system and it's concentrated in exactly the low-priced
     momentum names the strategy was steering toward. Part of why the price
     floor moved to $5.

  6. RETRYING AN AMBIGUOUS ORDER = DOUBLE POSITION. If an order call times out
     after the order actually went through, the natural move is to retry. Now
     explicitly banned; the agent reconciles against the broker instead.

  7. PARTIAL FILLS CORRUPTED EVERYTHING DOWNSTREAM. If the agent computes P&L
     off intended size while the broker filled 40%, every number in the
     learning file is wrong from trade one. Now: read actual fills back from
     the broker, always.

  8. THE LOSS LIMITS WERE UNREACHABLE. Original $3 daily limit vs a maximum
     possible loss of $1.50/day in Phase 1 — the rule could never fire. Scaled
     to $1.00 daily / $2.50 weekly so they actually work.

  9. CASH-ACCOUNT SETTLEMENT COULD FREEZE THE ACCOUNT FOR 90 DAYS. Resolved
     Aug 26 — your account is limited margin, so this risk doesn't exist. But
     it traded one exposure for another: a margin-capable account will open a
     SHORT if it's told to sell more shares than you hold, where a cash
     account would just reject it. That's why every order now carries its own
     idempotency key.

 10. THE AI COULD EDIT ITS OWN RULES. The skill said not to; nothing stopped
     it. Hence the chmod in Step 8, and "approval" being defined as you
     personally adding a dated row to the changelog.

None of these would have been obvious from watching it trade. They'd have shown
up as one unexplained large loss, weeks in.
